Problem

Media discovery fails to understand emotional context

Existing streaming platforms rely on keyword searches, genre filters, and algorithmic recommendations based on viewing history. But when users are looking for something to match their current emotional state, these tools fall short. You can't search for "something uplifting but not too intense" or "melancholic but beautiful."

01

Emotions are hard to articulate

Users struggle to translate their feelings into searchable keywords, leading to frustrating browsing sessions.

02

Recommendations miss the mark

Algorithmic suggestions based on past behavior don't account for current mood or emotional needs.

03

Decision fatigue is real

Endless scrolling through options leads to paralysis and dissatisfaction, even when perfect content exists.

Research

Understanding emotional media consumption

I conducted diary studies with 20 participants over 2 weeks, asking them to log their emotional state before and after consuming media (music, films, TV shows). I also interviewed psychologists who specialize in emotional regulation to understand the relationship between mood and media choices.

Key Insights

🎭

Mood regulation is intentional

People actively use media to shift or reinforce emotional states—to calm down, energize, process feelings, or escape.

🌈

Emotions exist on a spectrum

Users don't think in binary terms (happy/sad). They experience nuanced emotions like "nostalgic" or "contemplative."

⚡

Context matters more than genre

The same person might want a thriller when stressed but find it overwhelming when anxious—genre alone doesn't predict fit.

Solution

A mood-first discovery experience

MoodStream reimagines media discovery by starting with emotion. Instead of searching by title or genre, users navigate a visual mood landscape to find content that matches their current emotional state.

Design Concept

The Russell Circumplex Model

I based the interaction model on the Russell Circumplex of Affect, a psychological framework that maps emotions along two axes: energy (calm ↔ energetic) and valence (negative ↔ positive). This creates an intuitive 2D space for emotion navigation.

Users navigate a 2D emotional landscape to select their current mood

How It Works

A three-step discovery process

01

Select Your Mood

Users tap anywhere on the emotional landscape to indicate their current state. The interface responds with descriptive labels like "Calm & Content" or "Energetic & Joyful."

02

Explore Mood-Matched Content

Based on the selected mood, the app displays curated media organized by emotional themes and visual atmospheres rather than traditional genres.

03

Refine & Discover

Users can adjust their mood position or provide feedback to improve future recommendations. The system learns individual preferences over time.

Design System

Visual language that reflects emotion

I created a design system that uses color, typography, and motion to evoke emotional resonance. Gradient backgrounds shift based on mood selection, and typography scales dynamically to emphasize emotional descriptors.

Reflection

What I learned

Emotions are complex but mappable

The Russell Circumplex provided a solid foundation, but users also wanted quick access to specific moods (e.g., "nostalgic"). Balancing structure with flexibility was key.

Novel interactions need clear onboarding

Early prototypes assumed users would intuitively understand the mood landscape. Adding contextual hints and example selections improved comprehension significantly.

This concept has real potential

User enthusiasm validated the core idea. Next steps would involve partnering with content platforms and refining the mood-tagging algorithm with real data.
